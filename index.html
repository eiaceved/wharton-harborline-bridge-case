01

How this case works

This is an infrastructure concession case. A government leases an asset, like a toll road, bridge, airport, or parking system, to a private operator for a set number of years. The operator pays up front and keeps the profits. The bidder's question is simple to say and hard to answer: what are all those future profits worth today?

Wharton's casebook includes cases like this, where you drive the discussion: estimate revenue and costs, value the deal, then brainstorm risks and ideas. This is an original case written in that style; the city, bridge, and numbers are invented.

The one big idea: money later is worth less

Present value

  • An investor who wants 10 percent a year would pay about 91 dollars today for 100 dollars a year from now.
  • So 25 years of 60 million dollars isn't worth 1.5 billion today. It's worth about a third of that.
  • The case gives you a factor to multiply by, so you don't need a calculator.

Ten terms to drop naturally

Concession

A long-term lease of a public asset to a private operator, who runs it and keeps the revenue for a set term.

Up-front payment

The one-time price the operator pays the government to win the concession.

Required return

The yearly return an investor needs to make a deal worthwhile. Here, 10 percent. Also called the discount rate.

Present value

What future money is worth today, after discounting at the required return.

Annuity factor

A shortcut: the present value of 1 dollar a year for a set number of years. Multiply the yearly profit by it.

Perpetuity

A payment that lasts forever. Its value is the yearly amount divided by the required return.

Capex

Capital expenditure: one-time spending on equipment or upgrades, like new electronic tolling.

Weighted average

An average that accounts for mix. If 90 percent pay 4 dollars and 10 percent pay 12, the average is 4.80, not 8.

Operating leverage

When most costs are fixed, a small change in revenue causes a big change in profit.

Winner's curse

In a competitive bid, the winner is often the bidder with the most optimistic forecast, and overpays.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Port Halden, a mid-sized coastal city, plans to lease its Harborline Bridge to a private operator for 25 years. The operator would collect all tolls and pay to run and maintain the bridge. The contract also requires replacing the old cash toll booths with electronic tolling in the first year.

In exchange, the city gets one up-front payment. The city needs the cash, and it will pick the highest bid. Our client, Carver Ridge Infrastructure Partners, wants to bid. What's the most it should pay?

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

"So Carver Ridge wants to know the most it should pay up front for the right to collect the bridge's tolls for 25 years, after covering running costs and the electronic tolling upgrade. Is that right?"

Interviewer: "Yes." Naming both costs, running costs and the upgrade, shows you caught the details in the prompt.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your math or your bid.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • What return does Carver Ridge need on this investment?
    10 percent a year.
  • Can the operator raise tolls?
    Only in line with inflation. Assume tolls stay flat in today's dollars.
  • Who pays for major repairs?
    The operator. They're included in the yearly running costs.
  • Is there competition, for the bid or for the traffic?
    Two other funds are bidding. There's no other crossing today, though the city has discussed a tunnel.
  • What happens at the end of the 25 years?
    The bridge goes back to the city, with no payment.

The first answer matters most: the 10 percent return is what turns future profits into a price today. The tunnel comment is a risk to remember for later.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

How would you go about estimating what the concession is worth?

Your move

Take 60 seconds. Lay out how you'd get from traffic to a bid price, and what could change it.

Strong framework

What's the most Carver Ridge should bid for the 25-year concession?
1Revenue
  • Crossings a day × days a year
  • Toll by vehicle type, and the mix
  • Traffic growth over 25 years
2Costs
  • Yearly running and repair costs
  • One-time electronic tolling upgrade
  • Anything that changes after the upgrade
3Value
  • Yearly profit × 25 years
  • Discounted at 10 percent
  • Minus the up-front upgrade
4Risks and terms
  • Traffic, a competing tunnel, toll limits
  • Bid strategy vs. two rivals
  • Contract protections

Say it like this

"I'd value this like an investment. First, revenue: how many vehicles cross, what each pays, and how traffic might grow. Second, costs: what it takes to run and repair the bridge each year, plus the one-time tolling upgrade. Third, value: turn 25 years of profit into today's dollars at Carver Ridge's 10 percent return, and subtract the upgrade. That's the most it should pay. Fourth, I'd stress-test it for risks like lower traffic or a new tunnel, and think about how to bid against the other two funds. I'll start with revenue."

Why it works

  • It follows the money from traffic to a single bid number.
  • It names the 10 percent return early, which is what makes this a valuation, not a profit case.
  • It saves room for the brainstorm, which Wharton-style cases often end with.

05

Exhibit 1: traffic and revenue

Interviewer

Here's six years of traffic data. About 50 thousand vehicles cross each day, 90 percent cars and 10 percent trucks. Cars pay 4 dollars and trucks pay 12. Assume 360 days a year. What's the yearly revenue?

Your move

Get yearly crossings first. Then find the average toll, weighting for the mix of cars and trucks.

Hint: weighted average

Don't average 4 and 12 to get 8. Only 1 in 10 vehicles is a truck. Weight each toll by its share: 90 percent of 4, plus 10 percent of 12.

  1. 1 · Crossings a year50,000 × 36018M
  2. 2 · Average toll90% × $4 + 10% × $12$4.80
  3. 3 · Yearly revenue18M × $4.80$86.4M
  4. 4 · Trucks' share1.8M × $12 = $21.6M of $86.4M25%
  5. 5 · So what≈$86M a year; trucks punch above their weight; traffic +2% a year✓

Quick check: why isn't the average toll 8 dollars?

A simple average treats cars and trucks as if there were the same number of each. With 9 cars for every truck, the weighted average is 4.80. Everyday version: if 9 friends pay 4 dollars for coffee and 1 pays 12, the average spend is 4.80, not 8.

06

Exhibit 2: running costs and the upgrade

Interviewer

Here's what it costs to run the bridge each year once electronic tolling is in place. The upgrade itself costs 40 million dollars, paid at the start. What's the yearly profit?

Your move

Subtract running costs from revenue. Keep the one-time upgrade separate; it's not a yearly cost.

  1. 1 · Running costs$14M + $5.4M + $4M + $3M$26.4M
  2. 2 · Yearly profit$86.4M − $26.4M$60M
  3. 3 · Margin$60M ÷ $86.4M≈69%
  4. 4 · The upgradeOne-time, at the start: subtract it once$40M

Quick check: 25 years of 60 million dollars is 1.5 billion. Is that what the concession is worth?

Carver Ridge could earn 10 percent a year elsewhere, so 60 million in year 25 is worth only about 5.5 million today. The next step turns 25 years of profit into one number in today's dollars.

07

What's it worth today?

The core of the case: turn a stream of future profits into one price today.

Interviewer

Here's a table to help with the math. Assume flat traffic first. What's the most Carver Ridge should bid? Then tell me what happens if traffic keeps growing 2 percent a year.

Your move

Multiply the yearly profit by the right factor, then subtract the up-front upgrade.

Hint: why the factor works

At a 10 percent return, 1 dollar next year is worth about 91 cents today, the year after about 83 cents, and so on. Add up 25 years of those and you get 9.08. So 60 million a year is worth 60 times 9.08 today.

  1. 1 · Profits in today's dollars$60M × 9.08≈$545M
  2. 2 · Maximum bid, flat traffic$545M − $40M upgrade≈$505M
  3. 3 · With 2% traffic growth$60M × 10.61 − $40M≈$596M
  4. 4 · What growth is worth$596M − $505M≈$90M
  5. 5 · Sanity checkForever: $60M ÷ 10% = $600M. 25 years ≈ 91% of that✓

08

What if traffic disappoints?

Interviewer

Carver Ridge's partners worry the traffic forecast is too rosy. What happens to the bid if traffic comes in 10 percent below today's level?

Your move

Cut revenue by 10 percent, but think about whether costs move too.

  1. 1 · Revenue$86.4M × 90%$77.8M
  2. 2 · CostsMostly fixed$26.4M
  3. 3 · Profit$77.8M − $26.4M (−14%)$51.4M
  4. 4 · Maximum bid$51.4M × 9.08 − $40M (−16%)≈$426M
  5. 5 · So what10% less traffic → 16% less value: operating leverage✓

Why does a 10 percent traffic drop cut the value by more than 10 percent?

Revenue falls 8.6 million, and all of it comes off profit because costs don't shrink. Profit falls 14 percent, and the value falls even more because the upgrade is a fixed amount subtracted at the end.

09

Score the risks

Interviewer

Let's brainstorm. Here are six risks to the concession. Score each for likelihood and impact. Which three should Carver Ridge protect against in its bid or the contract?

Your move

Score each risk with a one-line reason, then name your top three and how you'd protect against each.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "Three risks matter most. Traffic coming in below forecast, so we bid on flat traffic and commission an independent traffic study. Toll increases being capped or blocked politically, so we want automatic inflation increases written into the contract. And a competing tunnel, so we ask for compensation if the city builds a new crossing during our 25 years."

    10

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    Carver Ridge's investment committee meets tomorrow. What should they bid?

    Your move

    Lead with a number. Then the reasons, the main risk, and what to put in the contract.

    Good answer

    "Carver Ridge should bid up to about 505 million dollars. The bridge earns about 60 million a year after running costs, which is worth about 545 million today at a 10 percent return, minus the 40 million upgrade."

    Better answer

    "Bid up to about 500 million, opening around 450 to 475. That's based on today's traffic: 60 million a year of profit, worth about 545 million at 10 percent, minus the 40 million upgrade."

    "Don't pay for growth. Two percent growth would justify about 596 million, but in a three-way auction the winner is often the most optimistic bidder. And because costs are fixed, a 10 percent traffic miss cuts the value by 16 percent."

    "Protect the downside in the contract: automatic inflation increases on tolls, compensation if the city builds a competing crossing, and clear repair standards. Next steps: an independent traffic study, an engineering inspection of the bridge, and financing terms."

    The good answer has the right number. The better answer explains how to bid, not just what it's worth, and turns the risks into contract terms.

    11

    Bonus drills: present value and brainstorming

    Short reps on the math and the creative part of the case.

    Brainstorm: raise revenue without raising tolls

    Interviewer

    The city won't allow real toll increases. How else could Carver Ridge grow revenue from the bridge?

    Your move

    Structure it: more traffic, better pricing within the rules, and new revenue streams. Aim for six ideas in 90 seconds.

    Strong ideas, organized

    • More traffic: partner with ports and freight companies to route trucks over the bridge; market to commuters as the fastest route.
    • Smarter pricing within the rules: peak and off-peak tolls that average out to the allowed level; frequent-user plans that lock in commuters.
    • Fewer missed tolls: electronic tolling with license-plate billing, so drivers without a tag still pay.
    • New revenue: lease space on the bridge for fiber-optic cables or cell equipment; sell anonymized traffic data to planners; roadside advertising where allowed.

    Quick present value reps

    1. At a 10 percent return, what's 100 dollars a year from now worth today?

    "About 91 dollars. 100 divided by 1.1. Two years out it's about 83, and three years out about 75."

    2. What's 5 million dollars a year forever worth, at an 8 percent return?

    "62.5 million. 5 divided by 0.08."

    3. If Carver Ridge needed 12 percent instead of 10, would its bid go up or down?

    "Down. A higher required return discounts future profits more, so they're worth less today. Try it in the calculator: it drops to about 430 million."

    4. Why does the bridge returning to the city after 25 years barely matter for the price?

    "Because profits after year 25 are worth very little today. Owning it forever would be worth 600 million against about 545 for 25 years, only about 10 percent more."
